# Flavour

Flavour is the combined impression of aroma, taste and the physical feel of food, not taste alone.

## Aroma

- Most of what we call flavour is smell; a blocked nose makes food seem bland.
- Aroma reaches the nose two ways: through the nostrils before eating (orthonasal) and from the back of the mouth while chewing and swallowing (retronasal).
- Heat releases aroma, so warm food smells stronger than cold food.
- Browning (Maillard reaction, caramelisation) and toasting create new aroma compounds.
- Fresh herbs, citrus zest and toasted spices lose aroma quickly; add them late.
- Fat carries and holds aroma, so blooming spices in oil spreads their smell through a dish.

## Taste

- The tongue detects five tastes: sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami.
- Tastes interact: [salt](./salt.md) reduces bitterness and enhances sweetness, acid brightens rich food, and sweetness softens sourness.
- Balance by tasting as you cook and adjusting one taste at a time.
- Fat is not a taste but rounds off sharp edges and extends how long flavour lingers.

## Other Sensory Factors

| Factor | What it is | Effect on flavour | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Appearance | Colour, shape, gloss and plating | Sets expectations before the first bite; browned, glossy or vivid food seems tastier | Golden crust, bright green blanched vegetables, glossy glaze |
| Sound | Noise made while chewing or breaking food | Crunch and crackle signal freshness and crispness | Crisp crackling, snapping fresh beans, crunchy fried coating |
| Temperature | How hot or cold food is when eaten | Alters taste and aroma strength; cold dulls sweetness and aroma, heat boosts aroma but can mask subtle taste | Ice cream tastes sweeter melted, soup needs more salt when served hot than cold |
| Texture | Mouthfeel: firmness, crispness, creaminess, chewiness | Contrast keeps a dish interesting; texture changes how fast flavour is released | Crunchy topping on soft purée, tender braise, creamy sauce |
