# Banchan

Banchan (반찬) are the small shared side dishes set out with a Korean meal of rice and soup. They aren't courses — everything lands at once, is eaten alongside the rice rather than before or after it, and is refilled freely. Most are made in batches and kept in the fridge, so a table of five or six dishes is assembled rather than cooked to order.

## Naming Key

Most banchan names are ingredient + method, so the suffix tells you how it was made.

| Suffix          | Method                                        | Example              |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| -bokkeum (볶음) | Stir-fried                                    | Myeolchi-bokkeum     |
| -jeotgal (젓갈) | Salted and fermented seafood                  | Ojingeo-jeot (squid) |
| -jorim (조림)   | Simmered down in seasoned liquid until glazed | Gamja-jorim          |
| -kimchi (김치)  | Salted and lactofermented                     | Baechu-kimchi        |
| -muchim (무침)  | Tossed raw or blanched with a dressing        | Oi-muchim            |
| -namul (나물)   | Blanched vegetable seasoned with sesame oil   | Sigeumchi-namul      |

## Building a Table

- Three to five banchan alongside rice and a soup or stew is an ordinary home spread; restaurants push it further.
- Spread the categories rather than the ingredients — one kimchi, one namul, one protein (jorim or bokkeum), one fresh muchim covers texture, temperature, and intensity at once.
- Salty, concentrated dishes (jangjorim, myeolchi-bokkeum, kongjaban) are eaten in small amounts as rice accompaniments; namul and muchim are eaten in larger amounts.
- Most are served cold or at room temperature, which is what makes the make-ahead batch cooking work.
- Kimchi and jeotgal keep for months and sharpen as they age; namul and muchim are at their best within a few days, so make those fresh around a standing stock of the fermented ones.
