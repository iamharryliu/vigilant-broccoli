## Montaigne's Stoves

| Lesson | Example |
| --- | --- |
| We mistake familiarity for superiority; the unfamiliar is not necessarily inferior, and the familiar is not necessarily universal. Exposure to other cultures tests assumptions. | In Alain de Botton's _The Consolations of Philosophy_, Montaigne found the French open fireplace and the German enclosed iron stove each judged superior by its users; he tried the stove and came to appreciate it. |

## Timeline

| Heating Method | How It Works | Pros | Cons | Region of Origin | Approx. Date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open Hearth Fire | Burning wood or other solid fuel in a central pit or ring of stones radiates heat to everyone nearby. | No equipment needed; doubles as light and cooking source. | Heavy smoke indoors; heat only near the flame; constant fuel gathering. | Africa/Eurasia | ~400,000 BCE |
| Kang (Heated Platform Bed) | A fire's flue gas is routed through channels under a raised brick or clay platform that is used for sleeping and sitting. | Fuel-efficient; warms the body directly; one fire serves cooking and heating. | Slow to heat up; risk of carbon monoxide and burns; tied to the building's structure. | Northern China | Neolithic (debated) |
| Ondol (Heated Floor) | A kitchen fire's hot gas flows through flues beneath a stone-slab floor topped with clay, heating the whole room from below. | Even, comfortable radiant warmth; reuses cooking fire. | Slow response; hard to retrofit; flue leaks can be dangerous. | Korea | ~1st millennium BCE |
| Hypocaust | A furnace sends hot air under a raised floor and up through hollow wall tiles, heating baths and wealthy villas. | Whole-room radiant heat; smoke kept out of living space. | Needed constant stoking by slaves or servants; costly to build; mostly for the rich and baths. | Roman Empire | ~80 BCE |
| Central Hall Hearth | A fire burns at the middle of a hall, with smoke escaping through a roof opening. | Simple; heat reaches all sides of the room. | Smoky and sooty; draughty roof hole; fire risk to thatch. | Northern Europe | ~500 CE |
| Wall Fireplace and Chimney | The fire sits against a wall under a chimney flue that draws smoke out of the building, allowing upper floors and smaller rooms. | Clear air; fire in any room; visible flame; flexible fuel. | Most heat goes up the chimney; draughts; uneven warmth (hot front, cold back). | Western Europe | ~12th century CE |
| Kotatsu | A low table frame with a heat source below is covered with a quilt so warmth stays around the legs and lap. | Very economical; heats people rather than the room. | Only warms those under it; fire or CO risk with early charcoal versions. | Japan | ~14th century CE |
| Tiled/Masonry Stove | A firebox feeds winding flues in a large mass of tile or brick that stores heat and releases it for many hours after the fire dies (Kachelofen, Russian _pechka_). | Very efficient; long, even, radiant warmth; burns little wood. | Heavy and expensive to build; slow to warm up; fixed in place. | Germany/Russia | ~15th century CE |
| Cast-Iron Stove | An enclosed iron firebox with a flue pipe heats the room by convection and radiation, with air intake controlling the burn. The "German stove" Montaigne encountered in the 1580s. | Far more efficient than an open fireplace; quick to heat; smoke contained. | Cools quickly; can scorch the air and feel stuffy; less visible flame. | Germany/Alsace | ~1500s CE |
| Franklin Stove | An iron stove open at the front, with baffles that route smoke downward before the chimney, combines fireplace view with stove efficiency. | More heat than a fireplace; keeps the visible fire. | Smoked badly in early designs; less efficient than closed stoves. | USA | 1741 CE |
| Rumford Fireplace | A tall, shallow fireplace with angled sides and a narrowed throat reflects more heat into the room and cuts smoke. | Much better radiant output than older fireplaces; less smoke. | Still loses most heat up the flue; requires careful construction. | Britain/Bavaria | 1796 CE |
| Hot-Water Central Heating | A boiler heats water that circulates through pipes to radiators in each room. | One fire heats the whole building; no smoke in rooms; steady heat. | Costly pipework and plumbing; freeze risk; slow to adjust. | Britain | ~1830s CE |
| Steam Radiators | A boiler's steam rises through pipes to cast-iron radiators, where it condenses and releases heat. | Strong output; suits large multi-storey buildings. | Noisy pipes; burn hazard; uneven heating without balancing. | USA | ~1850s CE |
| District Heating | A central plant sends steam or hot water through street mains to many buildings. | Economies of scale; can use waste heat or geothermal; no boiler in each building. | Heavy infrastructure; heat loss in pipes; depends on dense housing. | USA (Lockport, NY) | 1877 CE |
| Electric Resistance Heating | Current through a high-resistance element turns electricity directly into heat. | Clean at point of use; easy to install; precise control. | Expensive to run unless power is cheap; one unit of electricity yields one unit of heat. | Britain/USA | ~1880s CE |
| Forced-Air Furnace | A furnace heats air that a fan pushes through ducts to vents in each room, and the same ducts can carry air conditioning. | Fast heating; shared ductwork for cooling and filtering. | Dry air and dust; noise; drafts; ducts can leak heat. | USA | ~1930s CE |
| Heat Pump | A refrigeration cycle moves heat from outside air, ground or water into the building, delivering several units of heat per unit of electricity. | 3-4x more efficient than resistance heating; also provides cooling. | Higher upfront cost; output drops in extreme cold for air-source units. | Switzerland | ~1938 CE |
| Smart Thermostats | A networked thermostat learns schedules and occupancy, then adjusts the heating system automatically from an app. | Saves energy; remote control and usage reports. | Depends on network and app; privacy concerns; modest savings in practice. | USA | ~2011 CE |
