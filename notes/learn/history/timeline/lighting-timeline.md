# Lighting Timeline

| Lighting Method      | Approx. Date | Region of Origin  | How It Works                                                                                                                                                                |
| -------------------- | ------------ | ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Open Fire            | ~400,000 BCE | Africa/Eurasia    | Burning wood or other solid fuel releases light and heat as a byproduct of combustion.                                                                                      |
| Fat-Burning Lamps    | ~70,000 BCE  | Africa/Europe     | A wick (moss, fiber) draws liquefied animal fat upward by capillary action to sustain a small controlled flame.                                                             |
| Oil Lamps (Pottery)  | ~4,500 BCE   | Mesopotamia/Egypt | A fired-clay vessel holds plant or animal oil; a wick soaks up the oil and burns at its exposed tip.                                                                        |
| Candles (Tallow/Wax) | ~3,000 BCE   | Egypt/China       | A solid fuel (tallow or wax) surrounds a wick; the flame's heat melts fuel into a liquid pool that the wick draws up and vaporizes to burn.                                 |
| Rushlights           | ~500 CE      | Britain/Europe    | A dried rush pith is dipped in animal fat, which acts as both fuel and wick, and burns slowly when lit.                                                                     |
| Whale Oil Lamps      | ~1750 CE     | USA/Europe        | Refined whale oil burns cleaner and brighter than earlier fats, drawn up a wick in a metal or glass lamp reservoir.                                                         |
| Argand Oil Lamps     | 1780 CE      | France            | A cylindrical wick with air flowing through its center and around its outside gives more complete combustion and a much brighter, steadier flame.                           |
| Gas Lighting         | 1792 CE      | Britain           | Coal gas is piped to fixed burners, where it mixes with air and burns continuously at the outlet.                                                                           |
| Kerosene Lamps       | 1853 CE      | Poland/Germany    | Kerosene, distilled from petroleum, is drawn up a wick like oil lamps but burns brighter and cheaper.                                                                       |
| Arc Lamps            | 1870s CE     | Europe/USA        | High voltage across two carbon electrodes ionizes the air gap between them, producing an intensely bright electric arc.                                                     |
| Incandescent Bulbs   | 1879 CE      | USA               | Electric current heats a thin filament (originally carbon, later tungsten) inside a vacuum or inert-gas bulb until it glows white-hot.                                      |
| Fluorescent Lighting | 1938 CE      | USA               | An electric current excites mercury vapor in a sealed tube, emitting UV light that a phosphor coating converts into visible light.                                          |
| Halogen Bulbs        | 1959 CE      | USA               | A tungsten filament burns inside a gas that includes a halogen, which redeposits evaporated tungsten back onto the filament, letting it run hotter and brighter for longer. |
| Compact Fluorescent  | 1976 CE      | USA               | The same mercury-vapor/phosphor principle as tubular fluorescents, folded into a smaller tube that fits standard incandescent sockets.                                      |
| LED Lighting         | 1990s CE     | Japan/USA         | Current passing through a semiconductor junction causes electrons to fall to a lower energy state, releasing that energy directly as light.                                 |
| Smart/IoT Lighting   | ~2010s CE    | Global            | LED fixtures are paired with networked microcontrollers, letting brightness, color, and scheduling be controlled remotely via software.                                     |
