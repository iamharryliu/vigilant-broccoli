# Lighting Timeline

| Lighting Method      | How It Works                                                                                                                                                                | Pros                                                                            | Cons                                                                                        | Region of Origin  | Approx. Date |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ------------ |
| Open Fire            | Burning wood or other solid fuel releases light and heat as a byproduct of combustion.                                                                                      | No equipment needed; doubles as heat and cooking source.                        | Heavy smoke and soot; constant fuel gathering; open flame fire hazard.                      | Africa/Eurasia    | ~400,000 BCE |
| Fat-Burning Lamps    | A wick (moss, fiber) draws liquefied animal fat upward by capillary action to sustain a small controlled flame.                                                             | Portable; fuel is a byproduct of hunting, not a separate resource to gather.    | Smoky, strong odor, and dim compared to later lamps.                                        | Africa/Europe     | ~70,000 BCE  |
| Oil Lamps (Pottery)  | A fired-clay vessel holds plant or animal oil; a wick soaks up the oil and burns at its exposed tip.                                                                        | Reusable vessel; more controllable flame than an open fire.                     | Oil could be costly to produce; spill and fire risk.                                        | Mesopotamia/Egypt | ~4,500 BCE   |
| Candles (Tallow/Wax) | A solid fuel (tallow or wax) surrounds a wick; the flame's heat melts fuel into a liquid pool that the wick draws up and vaporizes to burn.                                 | Portable, no spill risk, easy to store and transport.                           | Melts/drips, shorter burn time per unit fuel, fire hazard if unattended.                    | Egypt/China       | ~3,000 BCE   |
| Rushlights           | A dried rush pith is dipped in animal fat, which acts as both fuel and wick, and burns slowly when lit.                                                                     | Very cheap, made from readily available plants.                                 | Burned quickly (minutes), needed frequent replacement, dim light.                           | Britain/Europe    | ~500 CE      |
| Whale Oil Lamps      | Refined whale oil burns cleaner and brighter than earlier fats, drawn up a wick in a metal or glass lamp reservoir.                                                         | Brighter and cleaner-burning than tallow or fat.                                | Expensive; tied to an unsustainable whaling industry; strong smell.                         | USA/Europe        | ~1750 CE     |
| Argand Oil Lamps     | A cylindrical wick with air flowing through its center and around its outside gives more complete combustion and a much brighter, steadier flame.                           | Much brighter and steadier than earlier oil lamps; more efficient combustion.   | More expensive and complex; required careful wick trimming and maintenance.                 | France            | 1780 CE      |
| Gas Lighting         | Coal gas is piped to fixed burners, where it mixes with air and burns continuously at the outlet.                                                                           | Centralized supply — no need to refill individual lamps; bright and consistent. | Fire/explosion risk, indoor air pollution, required piped infrastructure.                   | Britain           | 1792 CE      |
| Kerosene Lamps       | Kerosene, distilled from petroleum, is drawn up a wick like oil lamps but burns brighter and cheaper.                                                                       | Cheap fuel, portable, brighter than earlier oils.                               | Flammable, sooty smoke, distinctive odor.                                                   | Poland/Germany    | 1853 CE      |
| Arc Lamps            | High voltage across two carbon electrodes ionizes the air gap between them, producing an intensely bright electric arc.                                                     | Extremely bright; well suited to large spaces like streets and factories.       | Harsh, glaring light; high maintenance (electrode replacement); unsuitable for small rooms. | Europe/USA        | 1870s CE     |
| Incandescent Bulbs   | Electric current heats a thin filament (originally carbon, later tungsten) inside a vacuum or inert-gas bulb until it glows white-hot.                                      | Simple, inexpensive, instant-on, warm light quality.                            | Very inefficient — most energy is lost as heat; short lifespan versus later tech.           | USA               | 1879 CE      |
| Fluorescent Lighting | An electric current excites mercury vapor in a sealed tube, emitting UV light that a phosphor coating converts into visible light.                                          | Much more energy-efficient than incandescent; long lifespan.                    | Contains toxic mercury; flickering/hum; poorer color rendering.                             | USA               | 1938 CE      |
| Halogen Bulbs        | A tungsten filament burns inside a gas that includes a halogen, which redeposits evaporated tungsten back onto the filament, letting it run hotter and brighter for longer. | Brighter and more efficient than standard incandescent; compact.                | Runs very hot; still inefficient compared to LED.                                           | USA               | 1959 CE      |
| Compact Fluorescent  | The same mercury-vapor/phosphor principle as tubular fluorescents, folded into a smaller tube that fits standard incandescent sockets.                                      | Fits standard sockets; efficient and long-lasting versus incandescent.          | Contains mercury; slow to reach full brightness; disposal hazard.                           | USA               | 1976 CE      |
| LED Lighting         | Current passing through a semiconductor junction causes electrons to fall to a lower energy state, releasing that energy directly as light.                                 | Highly efficient, very long lifespan, durable (no filament or gas to fail).     | Higher upfront cost; cheap units can flicker or render color poorly.                        | Japan/USA         | 1990s CE     |
| Smart/IoT Lighting   | LED fixtures are paired with networked microcontrollers, letting brightness, color, and scheduling be controlled remotely via software.                                     | Remote control, scheduling, energy monitoring, integrates with home automation. | Depends on network/app reliability; higher cost; privacy and security concerns.             | Global            | ~2010s CE    |
